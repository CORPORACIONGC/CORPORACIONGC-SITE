Costa Rica now has rules for virtual asset businesses — and they come with a deadline. **[Law No. 10961](https://www.imprentanacional.go.cr/pub/2026/06/19/ALCA78_19_06_2026.pdf)**, published in the Official Gazette on **19 June 2026**, brought **Virtual Asset Service Providers (VASPs)** into the AML/CFT regime of Law 7786 and requires them, for the first time, to **register with SUGEF**, the General Superintendency of Financial Entities. The law **enters into force on 19 September 2026**. Fewer than three weeks remain.

If your company exchanges, transfers, holds in custody or markets virtual assets in or into Costa Rica — or if you are an international platform with local customers — this guide explains exactly what was enacted, who is covered, the obligations, penalties and realistic costs, and above all **what to do with the weeks that remain before the law takes effect**.

## The essentials, in one minute

- Law 10961 added **Article 15 quater** to Law 7786: VASPs are now **obliged subjects** under the AML regime.
- They must **register with SUGEF**. Registration "**does not represent an authorisation to operate**": the Costa Rican model is a registration with supervision, without a licence.
- Without registration, **no bank or supervised entity may maintain commercial relationships** with the company. The registration works as the key to the financial system.
- The law applies from **19 September 2026**, but the **CONASSIF implementing regulation is still pending**: there is no form, platform or registration window yet.
- Fines reach **50% of the transaction amount** and **one hundred base salaries**, and final sanctions are published.
- What can be done today: **scope diagnostic, corporate and tax adequacy, digital signature, banking strategy and the compliance programme**, so the file is submitted the day the window opens.

## What exactly Costa Rica enacted

Law 10961 — the product of legislative file 25.340 — adds Article 15 quater to Law 7786 and amends Articles 16, 16 bis, 25, 33, 33 bis, 81, 86 and 123. The chosen model is a **mandatory registration with SUGEF, coupled with risk-based supervision** of anti-money laundering, counter-terrorist financing and counter-proliferation controls.

It is worth fixing the exact nature of the figure from the outset, because the market is full of offers that describe it inaccurately. Article 15 quater says it in plain words:

> "Registration with the Superintendency does not represent an authorisation to operate."

The law leaves prudential regulation, market conduct and investor protection outside its scope. Any provider offering you a **Costa Rican "crypto license"** is selling a legal figure that current legislation simply does not contemplate. The correct word — in the proposal, in the contract and before the supervisor — is **registration**.

The registral nature of the filing makes it anything but a formality. The same article provides:

> "The entities described in Article 14, as well as the subjects registered under Articles 15, 15 bis, 15 ter and 15 quater, may not maintain commercial relationships where those carrying out the activities of this article are not duly registered with the General Superintendency of Financial Entities."

And the amended Article 81 sanctions the obliged subject that maintains commercial relationships with an unregistered VASP. Read it in operational terms: **registration is the condition of access to the Costa Rican banking system**. An unregistered exchange is, quite literally, left without an account.

## Who must register

Article 15 quater defines a VASP as any natural or legal person who, **as a business**, performs for itself or on behalf of third parties one or more of the following:

| Activity | Typical examples |
|---|---|
| **Exchange** between virtual assets and legal tender, or between forms of virtual assets | Centralised exchanges, crypto exchange houses, OTC desks |
| **Transfers** of virtual assets | Crypto payment processors, remittance services using virtual assets |
| **Custody, deposit, administration or control** of virtual assets by any means | Custodians, managed wallets, platforms controlling customer keys |
| **Financial services related to the issuance, marketing, offer or sale** of virtual assets | Token issuance platforms, placement of virtual assets for clients |

An exchange falls squarely under the first item and, depending on its model, under the second and third as well. The "as a business" condition excludes personal use and, as a rule, leaves out the merchant that simply accepts crypto as payment for its own goods or services — though the line becomes thin when that merchant habitually converts, holds or moves customers' assets. The words "for itself or for third parties" deserve care: a company that habitually buys and sells virtual assets with its own funds, as its line of business, sits inside the zone the statute covers even before it has a single customer. Operating "with proprietary capital only" deserves a scope opinion before it is ruled out.

**The connecting factor is territorial.** The law instructs SUGEF to ensure that

> "natural or legal persons, whatever their legal domicile or place of operation, who habitually and under any title carry out activities such as those indicated in this article without being registered, do not operate in Costa Rican territory."

A foreign structure serving the local market is covered. For groups with several entities — a Costa Rican company and a Panamanian one, for instance, a frequent arrangement in this sector — the first serious exercise is mapping **which activity is performed under which entity**, because that map determines who must register and what is described to SUGEF.

## The dates that govern the project

| Milestone | Date | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Publication of Law 10961 | 19 June 2026 | Done |
| Entry into force | **19 September 2026** | Running |
| CONASSIF implementing regulation | Up to three months | **Pending; no public consultation as of 1 September 2026** |
| Opening of the registration window | Depends on the regulation | No date yet |

The decisive fact today is the third row: **the CONASSIF regulation has not been published, and no public consultation draft is on record**. We verified this on 1 September 2026 against SUGEF's register of regulations under consultation, which lists only proposals unrelated to virtual assets. That regulation will define thresholds, categories of registered subject, the operational scope of each activity, the filing platform and the documentary checklist. Until it exists there is no form and no registration window — which is why **no firm can honestly guarantee a completed registration before September**. Whoever promises one is quoting a procedure that does not yet exist.

What can be promised — and it is where the difference will be made between operating smoothly and queuing behind the market — is **reaching 19 September with a complete file**, ready for submission the day SUGEF opens the process. As a reference for the pace to expect: under the procedure in force for the other registered subjects (SUGEF Agreement 11-18), the Superintendency verifies the file and notifies the applicant within a maximum of **ten business days**; where items are missing it issues a requirement with a ten-business-day response period, extendable once, which suspends the resolution period.

## The obligations under Article 15 quater

Items (a) through (j) impose the full FATF toolkit:

| Item | Obligation |
|---|---|
| a) | Customer and ultimate-beneficial-owner identification, with due diligence based on reliable independent sources |
| b) | Transaction records sufficient to reconstruct each operation, available on demand |
| c) | Controls over politically exposed persons (PEPs) |
| d) | Risk controls for new products, services, business practices and technologies |
| e) | Controls over virtual asset transfers, adapted to the technology |
| f) | Controls over third-party reliance for due diligence |
| g) | Controls and information exchange with foreign branches and subsidiaries |
| h) | Enhanced controls for high-risk jurisdictions |
| i) | Immediate suspicious-transaction reporting — attempts included — to the FIU of the Costa Rican Drug Institute |
| j) | Strict confidentiality over those reports |

The body of the article adds five structural burdens: a **documented and updated institutional risk assessment**, available to the authorities; the **FATF Travel Rule** — originator and beneficiary information on every inbound and outbound virtual asset transfer; **immediate freezing** of funds and prohibition of transactions with internationally designated persons; **keeping registration data permanently up to date** with SUGEF; and **contributing to the cost of supervision** under Articles 174 and 175 of Law 7732.

For a platform already operating to international standards, none of this is conceptually new. The real work lies in **landing it in Costa Rican form**: a compliance manual, risk matrix and procedures drafted against the local statute, in Spanish, defensible before SUGEF.

## Penalties: up to 50% of the transaction — published by name

| Conduct | Sanction (amended Art. 81) |
|---|---|
| Record-keeping failures in operations ≥ US$10,000 | Fine of **5% to 50% of the total transaction amount** |
| Failures in due diligence, PEP controls, FIU reporting or internal controls | Fine of **two to one hundred base salaries** (Law 7337) |
| **Refusing to register** | Fine of **two to one hundred base salaries** |
| Maintaining commercial relationships with unregistered subjects | Fine of **two to one hundred base salaries** |

Fines are payable within **eight business days**, with a 3% monthly surcharge, and **final sanctions are published** — adding a reputational cost in a business where correspondent banks and commercial partners screen sanction lists.

## The figures the filing requires — and the ones sold to you that do not exist

**Compliance officer.** Article 15 quater expressly makes it risk-based: SUGEF will weigh the size, structure, number of operations, headcount and risk exposure of the obliged subject to **require a compliance officer or authorise a differentiated structure**. For an exchange with meaningful volume, expect the requirement. By analogy with the regime in force for the other registered subjects (SUGEF Agreement 13-19), the likely profile includes appointment by the board, notice to SUGEF and the FIU within three business days, university-level technical education, three years of experience in compliance or in the registered activity, and disqualification for money-laundering convictions or designation on international lists. Two points that will save you money: **no rule requires the officer to come from your legal advisor**, and the role's independence argues for keeping the appointment in the company's hands.

**"Legal Manager".** This figure, frequent in offers aimed at international clients, **does not exist under Costa Rican law**. What exists is the **legal representative** (an attorney-in-fact registered with the National Registry, which every incorporated company already has), the **resident agent** — required only where no attorney-in-fact with sufficient powers is domiciled in Costa Rica, and who must be a Costa Rican attorney with an open office (Article 18(13) of the Commercial Code) — and the compliance officer described above. When you compare proposals, ask that every charged figure have a name and a basis in a Costa Rican statute.

## Roadmap: reaching 19 September with the file complete

Working backwards from the entry into force, a sound calendar looks like this:

| Phase | Content | Typical duration |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Diagnostic and feasibility** | Scope opinion under Article 15 quater, corporate and tax audit, digital-signature and banking verification, roadmap | 2–3 weeks |
| **1. Corporate adequacy** | Corporate purpose and bylaws if needed, powers of attorney, tax and RTBF registrations, digital signature, bank account | 3–4 weeks, in parallel |
| **2. Compliance programme** | AML/CFT/CPF manual, KYC policy, institutional risk matrix, PEP and sanctions procedures, Travel Rule, reporting, training, compliance-officer profile | 4–6 weeks |
| **3. Filing** | Assembly of the file, submission on the platform, response to requirements, follow-through | Per the regulation |

Phases 0 through 2 can be executed **today**, without waiting for the regulation, because their content depends on the statute already published and on the standard SUGEF applies to the subjects it already supervises. Phase 3 is calendared against the regulation, with a scope-review clause in case it moves the requirements.

### The three bottlenecks that decide the real timeline

**1. The digital signature.** Under the procedure in force, the application is filed through SUGEF's electronic platform **signed by the legal representative**, with filing through an attorney-in-fact confined to exceptional cases. The Costa Rican digital signature certificate is issued only to holders of a **national ID card or DIMEX** residency card. If every representative of the company is a non-resident foreigner, the filing stalls before it starts: workable routes exist, and verifying this is the first task of any serious diagnostic.

**2. The bank account.** Registration requires an account with a SUGEF-supervised institution, in the obliged subject's name and **dedicated exclusively** to the registered activity. For an exchange, opening and keeping that account is the single most material risk in the project: it turns on each bank's risk appetite. It is managed with a solid source-of-funds file and with candour; promising the outcome would be irresponsible, because no law firm controls the bank's decision.

**3. Tax coherence.** SUGEF will require an exhaustive description of the activity. In our practice we have found Costa Rican companies operating global platforms that appear before the Tax Administration as **dormant entities with no declared economic activity**. Presenting yourself to the supervisor as a VASP with that tax profile opens an inconsistency the supervisor will see. Regularising the tax registration and declaring the real activity is a phase prior to everything else — invisible from abroad, and detectable with a public-registry check almost nobody runs.

## Costs: what can be priced today, and what nobody can promise you

**The government component still has no serious figure.** The supervision levy for VASPs depends on the CONASSIF regulation and on the contribution under Articles 174 and 175 of Law 7732, and neither has been set for this sector. What can be quantified now:

- **Digital signature certificate:** a bank commission of roughly US$10 per holder, plus the card-and-reader device where applicable.
- **Registry and notarial certifications** of corporate standing, capital and powers.
- **Criminal-record certificates** from the country of birth, nationality and residence of every shareholder, participant and ultimate beneficial owner, **apostilled or legalised**, with official Spanish translations where issued in another language.
- The taxes the company already bears by existing: the **corporate tax** (Law 9428) and the annual **beneficial-ownership (RTBF) filing** with the Central Bank.
- **Professional fees** for the diagnostic, adequacy, compliance programme and filing — which in Costa Rica have a floor in the mandatory fee schedule of Executive Decree 41457-JP.

When comparing offers, the recommendation is simple: require every line item to distinguish what is **mandatory by statute**, what is **regulation-dependent** and what is **optional** — and be wary of anyone quoting closed figures for government fees no regulation has set.

## Checklist: the documents worth gathering now

**Corporate:** current certificate of corporate standing, articles of incorporation and amendments, capital structure, corporate books, financial statements, latest RTBF filing, corporate tax receipt. **Persons:** identification of shareholders, directors, representatives and ultimate beneficial owners, with apostilled criminal-record certificates from each person's three relevant countries. **Business:** an exhaustive description of the Article 15 quater activities performed and planned, volumes and jurisdictions, VASP registrations or licences held elsewhere, transaction monitoring system, Travel Rule provider, custody policy and source of working-capital funds. **Local infrastructure:** accounts with supervised institutions, a representative holding a Costa Rican digital signature, domicile and office in the country.
