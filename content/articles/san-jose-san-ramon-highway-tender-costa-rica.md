On 21 October 2026, Costa Rica's National Road Council (CONAVI) opened **international tender CRI-2026-2341-34907-O** to design, build, equip and maintain the widening of the **San José–San Ramón corridor**, the stretch of National Route 1 that links the capital with the country's western valley, together with the Río Segundo radial. Bids close on **21 April 2027 at 23:59**. The tender is split into two lots with a combined budget of **US$687.4 million**, financed by the Central American Bank for Economic Integration (CABEI, known in Spanish as BCIE) and the OPEC Fund for International Development, and it is run under **CABEI's procurement rules** instead of Costa Rica's General Public Procurement Law.

That last point changes almost everything a foreign contractor would expect from a Costa Rican public tender: where it complains, who decides, which securities it posts and which deadlines matter. This guide explains the legal framework as it stands on 19 November 2026, read from the bidding documents published by CONAVI, Law 10899, the loan agreements and the case law of the Costa Rican courts and the Comptroller General. It does not replace the bidding documents, which prevail, nor advice on a specific bid. A [Spanish version](/articulos/licitacion-corredor-vial-san-jose-san-ramon) is also available.

## The project and what is being procured

Law 10899 of 9 April 2026 approved two loans for the project: loan 2341 from CABEI, for up to **US$600 million**, and loan 16603P from the OPEC Fund, for up to **EUR 150 million**. The project's total cost is estimated at US$770 million, of which 89.3% goes to the works themselves and the rest to external supervision, audit, contingencies and administration (loan 2341, Annex F). The OPEC Fund agreement describes the scope: about 55.6 km of the corridor in six sections of two to four lanes per direction, 4.45 km of the Río Segundo radial to Route 147, 16 interchanges, 7 bridges widened and 5 new ones, 20 pedestrian bridges, 110 bus bays and 8 toll stations.

The contract is **turnkey**: the contractor designs, builds, fully equips and maintains the works during a term of **72 months**, split into 12 months of design, 48 of construction and equipment and 12 of defects correction. Prices are a lump sum and are **not adjusted** during execution; exchange-rate risk lies with the bidder, and only from month 72 may the contractor request an adjustment for pending work when the delay is not its fault (Bid Data Sheet 16.1).

Bids must cover all the functional units of a lot. A firm may bid for one lot or for both, and there is no cap on the number of lots a single bidder may win, provided it proves the capacity to perform both at the same time. CONAVI may also issue the notice to proceed for each functional unit on a different date.

## Which rules govern: Law 10899 and CABEI's policy

Costa Rica's General Public Procurement Law (Law 9986) excludes public loans from its scope, but adds that the purchases derived from them follow that law *“unless the law approving the loan provides for another procurement regime”* (art. 2 c, our translation). Law 10899 does exactly that. Its article 7 provides that these purchases are governed **exclusively** by CABEI's *“Policy for the Procurement of Goods, Works, Services and Consultancies with CABEI Resources”* and its implementing rules, and that this covers *“the entire procurement procedure, as well as the inherent remedies, such as the objection to the bidding documents and the challenge and/or appeal of the award, which shall be processed in accordance with CABEI's rules”*. The same regime applies to the OPEC Fund money, and the OPEC Fund agreement allows its procurement guidelines to give way to the co-financier's rules.

Two practical consequences follow. First, **each contract needs CABEI's no-objection**, from the bidding documents to the award and the contract (loan 2341, section 9.10). CABEI gave its no-objection to these bidding documents on 12 October 2026, while stressing that the technical and legal criteria are CONAVI's responsibility. Second, the tender runs on a **platform of its own**, CONAVI's tender portal, outside SICOP, Costa Rica's unified e-procurement system, and bids are submitted there electronically.

The bidding documents apply the complaints regime of CABEI's current procurement rules: complaints go to the borrower, the term is ten business days and the procedure is suspended while a complaint is pending.

Costa Rica's courts have long accepted this kind of arrangement. In 1990 the Constitutional Chamber held that a loan agreement may exclude certain national laws for its subject matter, that the lender's procurement rules may apply first and national law in a supplementary way, and that only rules of public order prevail against them (vote 1027-90). The same vote noted that admitting contractors from the lender's member countries on equal terms with Costa Ricans reflects the constitutional principle of equality.

## Who may bid

The tender is **open to companies from any country**. The bidding documents say so expressly: *“The Request for Bids is not restricted to bidders of a specific origin; national or international bidders from any country that are interested will be accepted”* (Bid Data Sheet 5.1, our translation). CABEI's members, as listed in the documents, are Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, Panama, the Dominican Republic, Belize, Mexico, Taiwan, Argentina, Colombia, Spain, Cuba and the Republic of Korea.

Origin still matters through the **regional preference**. When the highest-scoring bid comes from a firm of a non-member country, CONAVI checks whether any bid from a member-country firm lies *“within a range of up to 15% below”* that score; if so, it offers the best-ranked of them the chance to adjust its price to match the winning score, and moves down the list if that negotiation fails (Instructions to Bidders 6.1 b). For a joint venture, CABEI's rules look at the country where the lead member is legally constituted.

Other eligibility rules worth noting:

- **Joint ventures** may have up to three members, all jointly and severally liable for the whole contract (BDS 5.6 and ITB 5.10). A firm may join only one bid, though it may act as subcontractor in several.
- **Sanctions lists.** Firms listed by the UN Security Council, the European Union, the World Bank, OFAC, HM Treasury or CABEI's own List of Prohibited Counterparties are ineligible, as are those convicted of prohibited practices (BDS 5.3).
- **State-owned enterprises** may bid only if they are legally and financially autonomous and operate under commercial law (ITB 5.9).
- **Conflicts of interest** include common control between bidders, links with the firms that prepared the designs and family ties with the borrower's staff (ITB 5.5).
- **Origin of subcontractors and goods.** The Particular Conditions add origin rules for subcontractors, materials and equipment tied to the loan agreements (PCC 8.1).

## Qualification requirements

The technical bid is assessed on a **pass/fail** basis, and only bids that meet every criterion go on to price evaluation (BDS 35.4). The main requirements are:

- **General experience** of at least 15 years in transport infrastructure contracts, counted from June 2006.
- **Specific experience** in at least three contracts for widening or building highways with at least two lanes per direction and a minimum length of 15 km each, considered substantially complete at 80%.
- **Financial capacity**: audited financial statements for 2023, 2024 and 2025 under IFRS, average liquidity above 1.00, debt ratio of 0.70 or less, available financial resources equivalent to at least four months of the contract budget and average annual turnover of at least 40% of the committed annual payments. Foreign companies must have their statements apostilled or legalised, a step that may be completed after the award is notified.
- **Track record**: no contract default in the last five years, no penalties above 7.5% of a contract's value and no termination for environmental or social breaches.
- **Key personnel** with the experience set out in forms PER-1 and PER-2, and foreign firms must certify that they are in good standing with their professional associations at home.

Subcontracting may not exceed **40% of each lot's contract price** (BDS 20.2). The bid must be written in **Spanish**; supporting documents in another language are accepted with a reliable Spanish translation (BDS 11.1).

## How bids are evaluated

The tender uses **one stage and two envelopes**: a technical envelope, assessed on a pass/fail basis, and a price envelope, which CONAVI cannot open until the technical evaluation is over. Among the bids that pass, the winner of each lot is the one with the highest weighted score under a methodology set by the Ministry of Public Works and Transport (MOPT) and the Ministry of Planning (MIDEPLAN), as article 18 of Law 10899 requires:

- **Price, 90 points**, with the formula 90 × (lowest admissible price / bid price).
- **Life-cycle economic and social cost, 5 points**, calculated from the net present value of the proposed solution with assumptions set by CONAVI.
- **Resilience and operational efficiency, 5 points**, for design solutions that shorten the time needed to restore the road after disruptive events.

Prices may be quoted in colones for local inputs and in US dollars for foreign ones, but the comparison is made **in colones** at the Central Bank's buying rate (BDS 17.1 and 42.1).

## Securities, advance payment and taxes

<DineroSanJoseSanRamon />

The absence of a bid bond is the point that most surprises bidders used to Costa Rica's ordinary tenders. Its place is taken by a sworn declaration whose sanction is not monetary: the bidder that withdraws its bid is excluded from CONAVI's procedures for two years. If CONAVI considers a bid abnormally low or unbalanced, it may raise the performance security to as much as 20% of the contract price (ITB 41.3 b).

The tax exemption comes from article 13 of Law 10899. It reaches the purchases of CONAVI and of the contractors and subcontractors, *“provided such purchases are financed with resources from the loan agreements”*, applies only during the execution of the project and requires the beneficiaries to be current with the Ministry of Finance and the CCSS. Costa Rica's Attorney General has treated similar exemptions in earlier loan laws as objective exemptions, tied to goods financed by the loan and incorporated into the works (opinions C-150-1993 and C-034-2006).

## Complaints: what replaces the Comptroller General

In an ordinary Costa Rican tender of this size, the bidding documents are challenged by an **objection** before the Comptroller General of the Republic (Contraloría General, CGR) within eight business days of publication, and the award by an **appeal** before the same body (Law 9986, arts. 95 and 97). Under article 7 of Law 10899, this tender works differently.

<ReclamosSanJoseSanRamon />

The bidding documents set out a **complaints procedure** before CONAVI (ITB 48):

- **Against the bidding documents**, a complaint must reach CONAVI *“at least ten (10) business days before the deadline for the submission of bids”*. Prospective bidders count as interested parties for this purpose.
- **Against the notice of intention to award**, a bidder has *“a maximum of ten (10) business days”* from its notification. The notice must disclose the names and prices of all bidders and the reasons for rejecting each bid, and a bidder may ask for a debriefing within three business days.
- CONAVI must resolve each complaint **within ten business days** (BDS 48.5) and suspends the procedure until it does; when there are several lots, only the affected lot is suspended.
- **CABEI does not decide complaints.** It must be informed of each one and its solution, and it reserves the right not to finance a contract if the solution is late or does not serve the interests of the operation (ITB 48.8).

The bidding documents designate **no second instance** for complaints.

The role of the Comptroller General under this law is still an open question. The press reported in March 2026 that the CGR had stated it would not hear objections or appeals in this tender. Since 2024, however, the CGR has set out its own criterion on when a loan law takes these challenges away from it, in procedures financed by CABEI and other lenders (resolution R-DCP-00030-2024), and as of 19 November 2026 no resolution has applied that criterion to Law 10899.

Two points of history explain why bidders familiar with this corridor may expect something else. When the San José–San Ramón trust managed by the Bank of Costa Rica tendered the first urgent works in 2018 and 2019 under its own regulation, objections went to the CGR, which declared itself competent by virtue of its oversight role (R-DCA-0058-2019). And in the 2019 Inter-American Development Bank tender for the Barranca–Limonal section of the same Route 1, the loan law itself assigned objections and appeals to the CGR, which heard them (R-DCA-0813-2019). The courts, in turn, have reviewed awards in tenders financed by multilateral lenders, because public funds are involved, although we found no case in which an injunction against such a tender was granted (Contentious-Administrative Court, votes 627-2018 and 441-2024).

## The contract

The contract is **CABEI's standard design-build document**, one per lot, with general and particular conditions. It is not a FIDIC contract. Its main features are:

- **Governing law.** *“The law governing the Contract is the law of Costa Rica”* (PCC 6).
- **Disputes.** A permanent committee of experts with three members, which may include international professionals, decides within 42 business days; its decision binds both parties unless modified by an arbitral award, and becomes final if neither party gives notice of dissatisfaction within 28 business days. Patrimonial disputes may then go to **international arbitration under the ICC Rules**, limited to matters that do not involve *“waiver of public powers, public duties or disposal of public domain property”* (PCC 33.3 and 33.4). The Bid Data Sheet calls the same body a “DAAB”.
- **Penalties and termination.** Accumulated penalties are capped at 15% of each lot's authorised amount. At 6%, CONAVI must weigh, with reasons, whether to terminate; at 15%, it must start termination proceedings, after hearing the contractor.
- **Weather.** The particular conditions treat as force majeure, among other events, daily rainfall above 10 mm and rain lasting more than five hours (PCC 41).
- **Interface between lots.** Each lot's contractor is “Another Contractor” for the other. The Lot 2 contractor must integrate all its control, monitoring and communication systems into the traffic control centre at the Los Arcos toll station, in Lot 1, as a condition for putting Lot 2 into service.

## Integrity and prohibited practices

The loan agreement obliges Costa Rica to pass CABEI's integrity rules on to every contractor, subcontractor, consultant and bidder *“expressly in the contractual documentation”* (loan 2341, Annex H). The bidding documents define five prohibited practices (fraudulent, corrupt, coercive, collusive and obstructive) and a range of sanctions that goes from referral to the local authorities to the reimbursement of investigation costs; CABEI may also include the firm in its **List of Prohibited Counterparties**. Bidders must disclose commissions paid to agents and their beneficial owners (form CC-8), and CABEI keeps audit and inspection rights over the contract.

Law 10899 does not repeat the clause of earlier loan laws, such as Laws 10456 and 9144, which declared Costa Rica's regime of procurement prohibitions *“mandatory”*. Whether a national debarment under Law 9986 reaches a CABEI procedure has not been decided by the CGR or the courts.

## What Costa Rican law still requires

Being outside Law 9986 does not place the contractor outside Costa Rican law. Several requirements apply to anyone who builds in the country:

- **Commercial presence.** A foreign company that opens a branch must appoint a general attorney-in-fact who submits to Costa Rican law and courts for acts performed in the country (Commercial Code, art. 226).
- **CFIA.** Construction and consulting firms, national or foreign, must be registered with the National Registry and with the Federated Association of Engineers and Architects (CFIA Organic Law, art. 52). The CFIA's rules on firms allow a **temporary registration** to take part in a specific tender, at 25% of the ordinary fee, which must become definitive within ten business days of a final award. Foreign engineers and architects work as temporary members, and the bidding documents require them to join the CFIA when they join the works (BDS 51.1).
- **Social security and taxes.** Being current with the CCSS is a requirement to contract with any entity that manages public funds, and arrears during execution oblige the contracting party to withhold payments in favour of the CCSS (Social Security Law, art. 74). The bidding documents also require being current with the FODESAF social fund and the Ministry of Finance before signing.
- **Transparency.** CONAVI must publish on an open portal every procedure, the bidding documents, the status of the bids, the evaluation criteria, the professionals and firms hired and the contracts awarded, an obligation that, in the words of the law, *“may not be eliminated under any circumstances, even if CABEI disagrees”* (Law 10899, art. 7).

## Land, utilities and materials

The loans **do not pay for land**: expropriations and right-of-way purchases are borne by Costa Rica (loan 2341, Annex E). Law 10899 requires expropriations to be scheduled at least twelve months before each work front starts and makes substantial progress a condition for the notice to proceed, *“save in cases duly justified”* (art. 11). Utility companies must appoint a coordinator within three business days of CONAVI's request, and those that delay relocations bear the costs and the loan fees caused by the delay (art. 12). The Directorate of Geology and Mines grants the concessions to extract materials for the works (art. 15). The contractor also takes over the environmental licences and guarantees already issued by the National Environmental Technical Secretariat (SETENA) for the project.

## Who has bid on Route 1 before

Costa Rica's public procurement records show the market a bidder will meet. In 2019 the Inter-American Development Bank tender for the Barranca–Limonal section of Route 1, estimated at US$210 million, drew **eight bids**, from Chinese, Spanish, Mexican, Brazilian and Costa Rican firms and consortia. In 2025 CONAVI re-tendered the section under Law 9986 and received three bids for the first lot, including one from China Harbour Engineering Company; it awarded the two lots to a consortium led by BEL Ingeniería and to Constructora MECO, for a total of US$263.4 million including VAT, with no appeals filed. For this same corridor, the trust's 2019 prequalification for the urgent works admitted six consortia, most with Spanish partners, and the second lot was awarded in 2020 to a consortium of Constructora Hernán Solís and TPF Getinsa Euroestudios for US$17.5 million.

## How the corridor got here

The San José–San Ramón widening has been attempted for more than two decades. A concession was awarded to Autopistas del Valle in 2004 and endorsed by the CGR in 2005, assigned to the Brazilian group OAS in 2013 and terminated that same year after public protests over tolls. Law 9292 of 2015 then entrusted the project to a public works trust managed by the Bank of Costa Rica, which delivered only the first urgent works; CONAVI and the bank agreed on its early termination in August 2023, effective no later than the end of 2024. CABEI approved the new loan on 15 November 2024, the Legislative Assembly passed Law 10899 in April 2026 and CONAVI published the bidding documents in October 2026.

## Points to raise during the clarification period

The published bidding documents contain inconsistencies in dates, time limits, origin rules and the naming of the disputes body that a bidder will want clarified before the clarification period closes on 1 April 2027.
